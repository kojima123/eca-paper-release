## The problem
Can an LLM agent keep a task it has once solved as an ability that runs the next time without reasoning again, and go back to reasoning when that ability stops applying? Our control agent (Qwen3.8 Flash-Next on Daniel Franzen's milestone-2 harness) keeps no executable trace of its successes. In one fixed configuration it cleared 31 and 38 levels of the 8 public games in two runs; over 83 runs it cleared 45 distinct levels, but only 33.3 per run; in four runs of one configuration, 12 levels were cleared in some runs and not in others. With our built-in solver switched off, the model spends 0.36-0.51 M tokens per run on 13 levels the solver plays without generated tokens. Solving once does not make the next time cheaper.

Storing successes is not enough either. A stored procedure may not fit the present state: the board can look the same while the game's internal state differs, and an execution can depart from what was predicted. A stored procedure therefore needs conditions for when it may start, when it may continue, and when it must stop and hand back. Skilled people illustrate this division of labour: someone who can ride a bicycle does not think about pedalling, and starts thinking when something unexpected happens (Logan 1988; Schmidt 1975; Norman & Shallice 1986).

We evaluate in two settings: on the known public games, whether successes become routines and what reuse costs; on unseen games, how far routines apply.

## The approach: one execution contract
We started building this layer, ECA (Existential-Cognition Architecture), as middleware for physical AI, where a foundation model cannot be consulted at every control step. On ARC-AGI-3, ECA is the whole middleware between the harness and the game, including its gate, routine sources and supporting components. The paper evaluates the execution contract as a system; describing a component does not establish its effect in isolation. A **routine** may act without the LLM while its observations match its predictions (each source defines what it checks), and hands control back, with the position and the reason, at the first mismatch. A routine is one of two kinds:

- a **mechanic family**: one of 15 parameterised models pre-authored from the public games, certified when it predicts every transition recorded on the level so far and has a plan; a prefix executor then plays the plan under a lease, each action checked by the family's own verifier. They were the solver of the submitted kernels; here they are the fixed routine source for measuring the contract, not a proposed way to acquire models, and we claim no generalisation for them;
- an **instance replay**: a past success, harvested from the agent's own runs, verified by replay, keyed on the start frame's hash and checked by hash after every action.

The LLM is the supervisor: it acts on novelty and after a routine fails. Its successes are compiled into new routines between runs. Instance replay was evaluated in post-competition local runs and is absent from both linked submissions; host notes are used in later kernels, including the failure-note submission and local runs. The submitted kernel (ECAOD v1) is Franzen's notebook plus the family toolbox under this contract, run on demand. Three same-kernel hidden draws scored 36.14, 32.60 and 23.67; a fourth returned 0.00 with no log visible to entrants. The unmodified notebook scored 29.69 and 27.58. With a spread of about 12 points we claim no hidden-set improvement. The failure-note variant (30.68) also tells the LLM which family broke, where, and what it had predicted.

## Results (pre-registered; 8 public games; 60 min per game)
- **From success to routine.** The LLM cleared s5i5 L7 once, in 65 actions, 19 turns and 225 k tokens. Harvested and shortened to 61 actions, it then replayed in the next two runs at 0 tokens.
- **Replay of what was met before.** Replays matched 182 of 182 times at 0 LLM tokens. Levels per run rose from 34.5 to 45-46, the mean game score from 42.8 to 66.5-67.7, and tokens fell from 1.82 M to 1.34-1.38 M. The kernel also carried two harness fixes, so this is a system-level comparison.
- **Authority vs advice.** The same certified plans were offered to the LLM as one line of advice. The model followed 73-86 % of offers and cleared the same 13 solver levels, but every one of them then needed LLM turns: 76-85 turns, 235-252 k tokens and 71-90 game-minutes per run. When the solver held the authority it played 45 of 52 such level-runs with no LLM turn: 4-34 turns, 5-140 k tokens and 10-39 minutes over four runs, up to each game's last completed assigned level; these ranges leave out a level that could not be certified and that the LLM did not complete; counting it, that run's assigned levels used 309 k tokens, more than either advice run. The advice was one ~45-token line, so the cost lies in LLM turns, not prompt length.
- **Hand-back after a divergence.** A planted divergence stopped the replay where planted 16 of 16 times. Resuming from the board the replay had reached - a head start the immediate stop preserves - the LLM completed 11 of 16 levels, against 7 of 16 when it played them from the start, with a median of 41 model actions against 117. The head start, not the hand-back note, is the likely main factor, and at this n the difference is not significant (Fisher, p = 0.29).
- **Can the LLM write routines itself?** Under the same contract the LLM wrote the level's transition function and goal as code; the harness verified, planned and executed them (5 levels, 198 actions of its own). 49 of 91 searches found no completion plan within their scope and budget under a model consistent with every recorded transition, and 13 of 20 plans stopped at the first action reaching new ground. Levels fell from 69 to 55. Following every stop forward by model version (registered re-analysis): the 13 stops fell in 5 level attempts and each was followed by a new version; 3 attempts reached a version agreeing with every recorded transition, and two dc22 levels of one run were then completed by the harness in about as many actions as the LLM alone (40 vs 38/37; 72 vs 82/72). This is no gain over the controls, and model inadequacy was not separated from search cost.
- **First encounters.** On 33 unseen games (25 by NVIDIA, 8 by another author) the families certified twice. Each certificate was revoked after its first action, and the routines gave no level. Component contributions to the hidden scores were not isolated.

## Why the agent stopped
Each stop is logged at run time with the prediction, the observation and the stop position; candidate causes and separating measurements come from offline analysis of these records. Over 170,714 transitions, 19.8 % of repeated (game, frame, action) keys led to different next frames. So the frame alone does not carry the state within a level. A history of up to 6 actions separates 63 % of these keys, and on held-out runs it predicts 83.7 % correctly against 74.4 % for the frame-only majority, where it answers (40 %). At level changes, an effect-template predictor (no LLM) fitted on earlier levels predicted the next level's transitions exactly on 10.3 % of 1,240 transitions; 445 of its 1,112 failures were effects outside its class. Goals are the other gap: a completion test held on 0 of 114 clears.

## What it means
The control agent can find a solution and can read its history, but it keeps no procedure it can execute directly in the next run: the weights are fixed and the context is drained every ~20 requests. Verified routines persist, run exactly and generate no tokens, but do not yet generalise. Turning its own successes into routines that generalise is the open problem. In these runs, checked routines ran without generated tokens where they applied; when certification failed the cost returned to the LLM, and the LLM-written models we saw agreed with the record more often than they predicted the unseen part of a level.
